1. Learning objectives

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  1. Recall the core formulas of the Transformer from memory, with shapes.
  2. Answer common conceptual interview questions clearly in two or three sentences.
  3. Solve mixed numerical problems: attention, parameter counts, KV cache, compute, LoRA, quantization, decoding.
  4. Sketch an end-to-end design for an LLM application (a RAG assistant, a serving setup) and justify the choices.
  5. Identify your weak topics and know which section to revisit.

2. How to use this section

  1. Cover the answers. Try each question before reading the model answer — or use the flashcards below.
  2. Say answers out loud. Interviews reward clear, short explanations: definition → why it matters → one example or number.
  3. Do the numerical problems on paper. Then check them with the calculator and the drill below.
  4. Mark weak spots and go back to the section listed next to each question.
A good answer has three parts
  • What it is (one sentence).
  • Why it exists — the problem it solves.
  • A concrete detail — a formula, a number, or a real model that uses it.

5. Question bank with model answers

Click a question to reveal its answer — or practise with the flashcards after the list.

Foundations (Sections 1–3)

1. What problem did the Transformer solve compared with RNNs?

RNNs process tokens one after another, so they're slow to train and struggle to carry information across long distances. The Transformer uses self-attention, letting every token see every other token in one parallel step — fast on GPUs, with a one-hop path between any two tokens.

Section 1

2. Why sub-word tokenization?

Word-level vocabularies are huge and can't handle unseen words; character-level sequences are too long. Sub-words (BPE, WordPiece) keep common words whole and split rare ones into reusable pieces, so nothing is ever unknown.

Section 2

3. What does self-attention compute, in one sentence?

Each token's new vector is a weighted average of all tokens' value vectors, with weights from a softmax over query–key similarity.

Sections 3–5

4. Is self-attention aware of word order?

No — without positional information it is permutation-equivariant: shuffling the input just shuffles the outputs.

Sections 3, 7

Attention (Sections 4–7)

5. Why separate Q, K and V?

With one vector per token, tokens over-attend to themselves and relevance is forced to be symmetric. Separate projections let "what I look for", "how I'm found" and "what I pass on" differ.

Section 4

6. Why divide by √dk?

Dot products of random vectors have variance ≈ dk; large scores saturate softmax and kill gradients. Dividing by √dk keeps scores at variance ≈ 1.

Section 5

7. What does the causal mask do and why is it −∞?

It blocks attention to future positions so a decoder can't see the answer. −∞ gives weight exactly 0 while rows still sum to 1; a 0 would leave weight e0 = 1.

Section 5

8. Why multiple heads?

One softmax gives one weighting; several heads can track different relationships (syntax, coreference, nearby words) at the same cost, since dk = dmodel/h.

Section 6

9. What is GQA?

Grouped-query attention keeps all query heads but shares key/value heads among groups, shrinking the KV cache by h/g with little quality loss.

Sections 6, 14

10. Why is RoPE popular?

It rotates queries and keys by position-dependent angles so attention scores depend only on relative distance, has no parameters, and can be stretched to longer contexts.

Section 7

The block and architectures (Sections 8–11)

11. What do residual connections do?

y = x + F(x) gives gradients a direct path (∂y/∂x = 1 + F′), making deep stacks trainable, and turns each layer into a small edit of a shared "residual stream".

Section 8

12. LayerNorm vs BatchNorm?

LayerNorm normalizes each token's features independently; BatchNorm normalizes each feature across the batch. LayerNorm doesn't depend on batch size or sequence length.

Section 8

13. What does the FFN do, and how many parameters does it have?

It processes each token separately: expand to dff, non-linearity, project back. With dff = 4d it has 8d² parameters — about two-thirds of a block.

Section 8

14. Pre-norm vs post-norm?

Pre-norm, x + F(LN(x)), keeps a clean residual path and trains stably when deep; most modern LLMs use it.

Section 8

15. Encoder vs decoder?

Same block, different mask and objective: encoders are bidirectional and trained with masked LM (good for understanding and embeddings); decoders are causal and trained with next-token prediction (good for generation).

Sections 9–11

16. Why are most LLMs decoder-only?

One simple objective works on any text, every position gives a training signal, every task can be phrased as text continuation (prompting, in-context learning), and generation is efficient with a KV cache.

Section 11

17. What is teacher forcing?

During training the decoder is fed the true previous tokens (input shifted right) and predicts every next token in one parallel pass.

Section 10

Training and inference (Sections 12–14)

18. What are the training stages of a chat model?

Pre-training (next-token prediction on trillions of tokens) → supervised fine-tuning on instruction–response pairs (loss on responses only) → preference tuning (RLHF or DPO).

Section 12

19. What does AdamW add over plain SGD?

Per-parameter step sizes from running averages of the gradient and its square (with bias correction), plus weight decay applied directly to the weights.

Section 12

20. What is the Chinchilla result?

For a fixed compute budget, parameters and data should scale together — about 20 tokens per parameter. Models are now often trained much longer so that they're cheaper to run.

Section 12

21. Temperature vs top-p?

Temperature reshapes the whole distribution (sharper below 1, flatter above 1) without changing the ranking; top-p keeps the smallest set of tokens covering probability p and renormalizes.

Section 13

22. When would you use beam search?

When there's essentially one correct output, as in translation or speech recognition. For open-ended chat it produces bland, repetitive text.

Section 13

23. What is the KV cache and why is decoding memory-bound?

Keys and values of past tokens never change, so they are stored rather than recomputed. Each decode step must still read all weights and the cache to produce one token, so its speed is limited by memory bandwidth.

Section 14

24. What does FlashAttention change?

It computes exact attention in tiles using an online softmax, never storing the n × n score matrix — less memory traffic, O(n) memory, faster.

Section 14

Applications (Sections 15–18)

25. How does a ViT turn an image into tokens?

Split it into P × P patches, flatten each, project it linearly to dmodel, add a [CLS] token and position embeddings, and run a standard encoder.

Section 15

26. How does CLIP do zero-shot classification?

It embeds the image and text prompts ("a photo of a dog") in a shared space and picks the prompt with the highest cosine similarity.

Section 15

27. Explain LoRA.

Freeze W₀ and learn a low-rank update BA (B starts at 0); this trains r(d + k) instead of dk parameters per matrix and can be merged afterwards with no inference cost.

Section 16

28. Why does one outlier hurt quantization?

The scale is set by the largest value in a group, so one outlier stretches it and rounds ordinary small weights to zero. Group-wise scales and outlier handling fix this.

Section 17

29. RAG or fine-tuning?

RAG for knowledge (changing facts, private documents, citations); fine-tuning for behaviour (format, tone, a narrow skill). They are often combined.

Sections 16, 18

30. Why does continuous batching help?

Finished requests leave and waiting ones join every decode step, so batch slots stay busy instead of idling until the longest request ends.

6. Mixed numerical problems

P1 — Attention (Section 5). One query \(q = [1, 1]\), keys \(k_1 = [1, 0]\), \(k_2 = [0, 2]\), values \(v_1 = [1, 0]\), \(v_2 = [0, 1]\), \(d_k = 2\). Find the weights and the output.

Scores: \(1/\sqrt{2} = 0.707\) and \(2/\sqrt{2} = 1.414\). Softmax: \(e^{0.707} = 2.03\), \(e^{1.414} = 4.11\) → weights [0.33, 0.67]. Output \(= 0.33[1,0] + 0.67[0,1] =\) [0.33, 0.67].

P2 — Parameter count (Section 8). A decoder has \(d = 2048\), 24 layers, standard FFN (\(d_{ff} = 4d\)), a tied vocabulary of 32,000 tokens. Ignoring biases, norms and positions, how many parameters?

Blocks: \(12 \times 2048^2 \times 24 = 1{,}207{,}959{,}552\). Embeddings: \(32{,}000 \times 2048 = 65{,}536{,}000\). Total 1,273,495,552 ≈ 1.27B.

P3 — KV cache (Section 14). The same model uses 16 heads of \(d_{head} = 128\) (standard multi-head attention), 16-bit cache. Cache per token and for an 8,192-token context?

\(2 \times 24 \times 16 \times 128 \times 2 =\) 196,608 bytes per token; × 8,192 = 1,610,612,736 bytes = 1.5 GiB.

P4 — Compute (Section 12). How many tokens would be "Chinchilla-optimal" for this model, and how much training compute?

\(D \approx 20N = 20 \times 1.27 \times 10^9 \approx\) 25.5B tokens. \(C \approx 6ND = 6 \times 1.27 \times 10^9 \times 2.55 \times 10^{10} \approx\) 1.9 × 10²⁰ FLOPs.

P5 — LoRA (Section 16). LoRA with \(r = 8\) on \(W_Q\) and \(W_V\) (\(2048 \times 2048\) each) in all 24 layers. Trainable parameters?

\(24 \times 2 \times 8 \times (2048 + 2048) =\) 1,572,864 ≈ 0.12% of the model.

P6 — Quantization (Section 17). Weight memory of this model in 16-bit and in INT4? Lower bound on decode time per token at batch 1 on a 2 TB/s GPU (16-bit)?

16-bit: \(1.27 \times 10^9 \times 2 =\) 2.5 GB; INT4: 0.64 GB. Time ≥ \(2.55 \times 10^9 / 2 \times 10^{12} \approx\) 1.3 ms/token (≈ 790 tokens/s at most).

P7 — Decoding (Section 13). Logits [2, 1, 0] at temperature 0.5?

Divide by 0.5 → [4, 2, 0]; \(e^4 = 54.6\), \(e^2 = 7.39\), \(e^0 = 1\) → [0.867, 0.117, 0.016].

P8 — Loss (Section 12). A model's average cross-entropy on a test set is 2.0 nats per token. Perplexity?

\(e^{2.0} =\) 7.39 — as unsure as choosing among about 7 tokens.

P9 — Positions (Section 7). Sinusoidal encoding for position 2 with \(d_{model} = 4\)?

\([\sin 2, \cos 2, \sin 0.02, \cos 0.02] =\) [0.909, −0.416, 0.020, 1.000].

P10 — ViT (Section 15). A 448 × 448 image with 14 × 14 patches: how many patches, and how does attention cost compare with 196 patches?

\((448/14)^2 = 32^2 =\) 1,024 patches; cost ratio \((1024/196)^2 \approx\) 27×.

7. System-design questions

7.1 "Design a question-answering assistant over our college's circulars."

A strong answer covers:

  1. Requirements: questions in English (and maybe Telugu/Hindi), answers must cite the circular, data changes weekly, students may only see documents meant for them.
  2. Indexing: extract text from PDFs → chunk (~300–500 tokens, ~10% overlap) → embed with a multilingual embedding model → store vectors + text + metadata (date, department, audience) in a vector database; re-index new circulars automatically.
  3. Retrieval: hybrid search (BM25 for exact terms like course codes + vectors for meaning), filter by the student's access rights and date, re-rank the top ~20 with a cross-encoder, keep the top ~5.
  4. Generation: a prompt that says "answer only from the context, cite [n], say you don't know otherwise"; a mid-sized instruction-tuned LLM.
  5. Evaluation: a test set of real student questions with known answers; track recall@5, faithfulness and answer correctness; monitor "I don't know" rates.
  6. Risks: outdated circulars (prefer the newest by date), missing documents, prompt injection inside documents (treat retrieved text as data, not instructions).

7.2 "Serve a 7B chat model to 1,000 concurrent users."

A strong answer covers:

  1. Memory: 13.5 GB weights in 16-bit (or ~4 GB in 4-bit); KV cache 0.5 MB/token for LLaMA-2-style MHA — GQA models need far less. 1,000 users × 2,000 tokens × 0.5 MB = 1 TB of cache in the worst case, so you need GQA, an 8-bit cache, and several GPUs or replicas.
  2. Engine: vLLM/TGI-style continuous batching with PagedAttention and prefix caching for the shared system prompt.
  3. Latency targets: TTFT under ~1 s, TPOT ~30–50 ms; stream tokens; autoscale replicas on queue length.
  4. Cost: measure tokens/s per GPU and compute cost per million tokens; consider quantization and speculative decoding.
  5. Safety and reliability: rate limits, input/output filtering, logging, fallbacks when a replica fails.

9. Common interview mistakes

  • Reciting buzzwords without the "why". Always say what problem a technique solves.
  • Mixing up training and inference. Training is parallel (teacher forcing); generation is sequential (one pass per token, with a KV cache).
  • Forgetting shapes. Interviewers often ask "what's the shape of \(QK^{\top}\)?" — answer \(n \times n\), and say why that's the \(O(n^2)\) cost.
  • Over-claiming. Say "reduces" hallucination (RAG), "usually small" accuracy loss (4-bit), "roughly" 20 tokens per parameter.
  • Ignoring cost and latency in design questions. Mention memory, throughput and cost per token.

10. Summary

  • The whole course rests on a few formulas: embeddings, \(\text{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k} + M)V\), multi-head concatenation, residual + norm + FFN blocks, and next-token cross-entropy.
  • A handful of numbers (\(12d^2\) per block, 16 bytes/param for training, \(6ND\), 0.5 MB/token KV cache for a 7B MHA model, ~20 tokens/param) let you sanity-check almost any claim.
  • Good answers follow what → why → a concrete detail.
  • Design answers should cover requirements, data/memory, the pipeline, evaluation, cost and risks.

11. Final self-test

  1. In one sentence each, explain: the KV cache, LoRA, and RAG.
  2. A model has \(d = 4096\), 32 layers, tied vocabulary 32,000, standard FFN. Estimate its parameters (ignore biases and norms).
  3. Why can't you train a bidirectional encoder with next-token prediction?
Show answers
  1. KV cache: store each past token's keys and values so each generation step computes projections only for the new token. LoRA: freeze the weights and train a small low-rank update \(BA\) that can be merged afterwards. RAG: retrieve relevant documents at question time and put them in the prompt so the model answers from them with citations.
  2. \(12 \times 4096^2 \times 32 = 6{,}442{,}450{,}944\) plus \(32{,}000 \times 4096 = 131{,}072{,}000\) → ≈ 6.57B. (LLaMA-2 7B is 6.74B because it uses a SwiGLU FFN with \(d_{ff} = 11{,}008\) and a separate output head — set those in the capstone calculator to see 6.74B.)
  3. Without a causal mask, each position can attend directly to the next token — the one it's supposed to predict — so the model would just copy it and learn nothing useful. Encoders use masked LM instead.
